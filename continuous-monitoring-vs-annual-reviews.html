Continuous Monitoring vs. Annual Reviews: What Actually Reduces Risk?

October 2026·10 min read·Gabriel Hasik

Management Summary

Annual supplier reviews are comfortable because they create a visible cycle. They are also dangerously slow for critical suppliers. A supplier can change its infrastructure, lose key staff, suffer an incident, add subcontractors, or become financially stressed months before the next review date. If the organization only looks once a year, risk is managed through memory and luck.

Continuous monitoring does not mean watching everything all the time. That would create noise. It means identifying the risk signals that matter for the supplier's role and creating triggers that force review when the risk profile changes.

Core point: Annual reviews prove that a process exists. Continuous monitoring helps detect when a decision should change.

1. What Annual Reviews Are Good For

Annual reviews still have value. They create discipline, refresh evidence, confirm ownership, and provide a predictable moment to reassess the relationship. They are useful for standard suppliers and for governance reporting. The mistake is treating them as sufficient for critical dependencies.

2. What Changes Between Reviews

Risk changes when services expand, data categories change, privileged access is granted, subcontractors are introduced, control reports expire, vulnerabilities are disclosed, incidents occur, financial distress appears, or regulatory expectations shift. A mature program catches these changes through triggers, not calendar reminders alone.

SignalReview trigger
New data or accessReassess inherent risk and evidence needs.
Expired assurance reportRequest refresh or compensating evidence.
Supplier incidentAssess exposure, notification duties, and remediation.
New subcontractorReview concentration and nth-party dependency.

3. Avoid Monitoring Theatre

Some monitoring programs collect too many signals: ratings, news alerts, domain findings, vulnerability mentions, financial indicators, and generic threat intelligence. More data does not automatically create better risk management. If alerts are not tied to decisions, they become another inbox.

The useful question is: what would we do differently if this signal changed? If the answer is unclear, the signal should not be in the first version of the program.

4. Product Implication

A lightweight TPRM product should support event-based review. It should show when evidence expires, when criticality changes, when a finding becomes overdue, when a supplier incident is recorded, and when a business owner needs to make a decision. The product should turn monitoring into workflow, not noise.

Practical next step: Define five triggers that require reassessment for critical suppliers. Start there before buying another monitoring feed.
