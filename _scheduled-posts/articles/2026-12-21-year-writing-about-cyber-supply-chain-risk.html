What I Learned From a Year of Writing About Cyber Supply Chain Risk

December 2026·9 min read·Gabriel Hasik

Management Summary

Writing about cyber supply chain risk throughout the year has reinforced one thing: the topic is no longer a specialist corner of cybersecurity. It sits across security, procurement, legal, privacy, resilience, audit, and management. Everyone touches it, and that is exactly why it is so hard to govern.

The most interesting pattern is not that organizations lack effort. Many teams work very hard. The problem is that effort is often fragmented. Supplier data is incomplete, evidence is disconnected from decisions, and ownership is spread across functions that do not always share the same language.

Core point: The next step in supply chain risk is not more paperwork. It is clearer operating models and better risk intelligence.

1. Compliance Created Momentum

NIS2, DORA, and other regulatory expectations helped push supplier risk into management conversations. That is useful. Regulation creates urgency and budget. But compliance alone cannot make a program effective. The real question is whether the organization understands its dependencies better than before.

2. The Supplier Inventory Is Still the Hard Part

Again and again, the basic question remains difficult: which suppliers matter most and why? Until that is answered, the rest of the program is built on uncertain ground. A supplier inventory must connect suppliers to services, business owners, data, access, contracts, and issues.

3. The Questionnaire Is Overloaded

Questionnaires are asked to do too much. They are used for discovery, assurance, evidence collection, audit trail, supplier education, and decision support. That overload creates fatigue. The better pattern is to use questionnaires as one input into a broader workflow.

4. The Product Gap Is Real

Many organizations do not need a giant platform. They need help with the practical middle: classification, evidence tracking, issue ownership, review cadence, and management reporting. This is where a focused product could create real value.

5. What I Would Watch in 2027

I would watch three areas: supply chain concentration, regulatory pressure on evidence quality, and the move from annual reviews to event-based supplier monitoring. Organizations that can turn supplier data into decisions will be better positioned than those that simply collect more files.

Practical next step: Use the year-end period to identify the five supplier risk questions your organization still cannot answer quickly. Those questions should shape next year's program.
